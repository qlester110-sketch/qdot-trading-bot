## Section 1 — The reality check (read this first)

Before building knowledge, you need to know what the data says about people who try to do this. This isn't a discouragement; it's calibration. Plenty of people build wealth through markets. Almost none of them do it the way it's marketed.

### What the academic record shows

The most-cited dataset on retail performance is Brad Barber and Terrance Odean's work, which tracked tens of thousands of individual brokerage accounts. Across multiple papers, the conclusions are consistent:

- The average individual investor underperforms a simple market index by roughly 1.5% per year.
- The most active traders underperform by about 6.5% annually.
- Among day traders specifically, only about 1% are predictably profitable after fees over time.
- Roughly 80% of day traders quit within two years; only about 7% are still trading after five.
- Frequent traders (500+ trades/year) lose at rates approaching 80%.

A 2025 PiP World longitudinal study covering 8 million traders and 295 million trades from 1998–2025 found that 74–89% of retail traders lost money, and that the failure rate has not improved across nearly three decades despite better tools, education, and apps.

The pattern is the same across markets — equities, FX, futures, crypto. The pattern is also the same across "education levels" — having read more books or watched more YouTube does not move the failure rate.

### Why this happens (the four mechanical reasons)

1. **Costs.** Frequent trading generates commissions, spreads, slippage, and short-term tax rates. Even a strategy that's 50/50 before costs becomes a guaranteed loser after costs.
2. **Overconfidence.** Investors systematically overestimate their skill. Studies show traders continue trading after 10+ years of negative returns, blaming the market rather than their process.
3. **Disposition effect.** People sell winners at a 50% higher rate than losers. We lock in small gains and let losses run, which is the inverse of what risk management requires.
4. **Selection bias in what you see.** Survivorship bias means the only traders you hear about online are the ones who haven't blown up yet. The 80% who quit don't tweet about it.

### What this means for you

Treat the next sections as building a defensible long-term position first, *then* deciding if you want to allocate a small "exploration" sleeve to active strategies. The order matters. Most people skip the boring part because the exciting part is louder, and that's how they end up in the 74–89%.

**Do next:**
- Read: ["The Data on Day Trading"](https://www.currentmarketvaluation.com/posts/the-data-on-day-trading.php) — summarizes the academic record concisely.
- Read: Barber & Odean, "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth" (2000) — searchable on SSRN, free.

## Section 2 — The vocabulary you need before anything else

You can't evaluate strategies without knowing the language. Spend a week here. Don't move on until each term reads as obvious.

### Asset classes

- **Equities (stocks)** — ownership stakes in companies. Returns come from price appreciation and dividends. Higher long-term return, higher volatility.
- **Fixed income (bonds)** — loans to governments or corporations. Returns from coupon payments and price changes. Lower return, lower volatility, generally moves opposite stocks during normal recessions.
- **Cash and cash equivalents** — money markets, T-bills, high-yield savings. Preservation of capital, not growth.
- **Real assets** — real estate, commodities, infrastructure. Inflation hedges, different return drivers.
- **Alternatives** — private equity, hedge funds, crypto. Higher complexity, often higher fees, often illiquid.

### Investment vehicles

- **Individual stocks** — direct ownership of one company. Maximum upside, no diversification.
- **Mutual funds** — pooled investments, priced once daily, often actively managed (and expensive).
- **ETFs (exchange-traded funds)** — pooled investments that trade like stocks intraday, usually tracking an index, usually cheaper than mutual funds.
- **Index funds** — funds that mechanically replicate an index (e.g., S&P 500). Low cost, no manager picking stocks.
- **Options** — contracts giving the right (not obligation) to buy/sell at a set price. Powerful, leveraged, easy to lose 100%.
- **Futures** — obligations to buy/sell at a set price/date. Heavily leveraged, used for hedging and speculation.

### Market structure terms

- **Bid/ask/spread** — what buyers offer, what sellers ask, the gap between. The spread is your friction cost.
- **Volume / liquidity** — how much trades. Thin volume means worse prices.
- **Volatility** — how much prices move. Often measured as standard deviation of returns.
- **Drawdown** — peak-to-trough decline. A 50% drawdown requires a 100% gain to recover.
- **Beta** — sensitivity to market moves. Beta of 1 = moves with market; 2 = twice as much.
- **Alpha** — return above what beta would predict. The thing every active manager claims to deliver and most don't.

### Account types (US)

- **Brokerage (taxable)** — flexible, but every sale is a taxable event.
- **Traditional IRA / 401(k)** — pre-tax contributions, taxed on withdrawal. Reduces today's tax bill.
- **Roth IRA / Roth 401(k)** — post-tax contributions, withdrawals tax-free. Better when you expect higher future tax rates.
- **HSA** — triple tax-advantaged if used for medical: deductible going in, growth tax-free, withdrawals tax-free for medical. After 65 it acts like a traditional IRA for non-medical.

- Open Investopedia and read the "Investing Essentials" series — solid, free, ad-supported but accurate.
- Bookmark the SEC's [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing). Read the entire Beginner's Guide section.

## Section 3 — The framework: passive core, active satellite

Before any strategy, you need to choose a framework. The mainstream evidence-based framework — and the one most professional advisors actually use for their own money — is **core/satellite**:

- **Core (most of your money):** Low-cost, broadly diversified index funds. Captures market returns. Boring. Works.
- **Satellite (a small percentage):** Whatever active or specific bets you want to make. Individual stocks, sector tilts, options, whatever. Capped so a blow-up doesn't matter.

The reason this framework dominates is that it concedes the academic finding (markets are mostly efficient and most active managers underperform after fees) while leaving room for the human reality that you'll want to do *something* active anyway. Better to give that impulse a small, walled-off sandbox than to let it run your whole portfolio.

### The Bogleheads three-fund portfolio (the canonical core)

Built around three index funds:
1. **Total US Stock Market** (e.g., VTI, FZROX, SCHB)
2. **Total International Stock** (e.g., VXUS, FZILX, SCHF)
3. **Total US Bond Market** (e.g., BND, FXNAX, SCHZ)

Allocation depends on age and risk tolerance. Common rules of thumb:
- Aggressive (long horizon): 60% US / 20% Intl / 20% Bonds, or 70/20/10
- Moderate: 50/30/20
- Conservative: "age in bonds" — at 35, that's 35% bonds

Backtest data from this portfolio over ~30 years shows ~7.9% compound annual return with ~12.5% standard deviation and a worst drawdown of ~44% (2008–09 area). That's the *real* shape of long-term equity returns: good on average, brutal in the bad years, requires you to not panic sell.

**Why three funds and not one S&P 500 fund?** International diversification cuts country-specific risk. Bonds reduce volatility and provide rebalancing dry powder during crashes. The S&P 500 alone is essentially a bet on US large caps continuing to dominate, which they have, but historically not always.

### The arguments against passive

You should know these so you don't fall for them when someone with a course to sell brings them up:
- *"Indexing only works in bull markets"* — Indexes have outperformed the average active manager in bear markets too. Active managers don't reliably time exits.
- *"You can do better"* — Statistically true for ~1% over decades. Not statistically distinguishable from luck for the rest.
- *"This time is different / valuations are stretched"* — This argument has been made every year since 2010. The market has more than tripled.

### Where passive can fail

It's not magic. It assumes:
- You stay invested through 30–50% drawdowns without selling.
- Your time horizon actually is long.
- You don't need the money during a bad sequence of returns near retirement.

If you can't do those things, you need either a more conservative allocation or a different strategy entirely (e.g., goal-based bucketing).

**Do next:**
- Read the [Bogleheads wiki: Three-fund portfolio](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Three-fund_portfolio) start to finish.
- Read: *The Bogleheads' Guide to the Three-Fund Portfolio* by Taylor Larimore (~75 pages, fast read).
- Read: *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing* by John Bogle.

## Section 4 — Reading list, ordered

The internet has effectively infinite content on investing. 95%+ is noise. Here's a tight, ordered list of what to read and why. Tackle them in roughly this order.

### Tier 1 — Foundations (read these no matter what)

1. **The Little Book of Common Sense Investing — John Bogle.** The case for index funds from the founder of Vanguard. ~200 pages. Read first.
2. **The Bogleheads' Guide to Investing — Taylor Larimore et al.** Practical Boglehead playbook. Will answer 90% of beginner questions.
3. **A Random Walk Down Wall Street — Burton Malkiel.** The argument for market efficiency, written readably. Now in 13th+ edition.
4. **The Intelligent Investor — Benjamin Graham.** 1949, still the foundational value-investing text. Buffett's "by far the best book on investing ever written." Skip the chapters on specific 1940s securities; read Chapters 8 and 20 carefully.

### Tier 2 — Mental models and behavior

5. **Thinking, Fast and Slow — Daniel Kahneman.** Not a finance book. The single most important book for understanding why your brain will sabotage your investing.
6. **Fooled by Randomness — Nassim Taleb.** Survivorship bias, luck vs. skill, why most "track records" are noise.
7. **The Psychology of Money — Morgan Housel.** Short, well-written, behavioral.
8. **Misbehaving — Richard Thaler.** Behavioral economics from a Nobel laureate.

### Tier 3 — If you want to go deeper into stock-picking

9. **Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits — Philip Fisher.** Qualitative analysis, "scuttlebutt" approach.
10. **One Up On Wall Street — Peter Lynch.** Buy what you understand. Underrated for beginners.
11. **The Most Important Thing — Howard Marks.** Risk, second-level thinking. Marks's memos (free at Oaktree) are required reading after this.
12. **The Essays of Warren Buffett — ed. Lawrence Cunningham.** Buffett's annual letters, organized by topic.

### Tier 4 — Markets, history, and macro

13. **Stocks for the Long Run — Jeremy Siegel.** Long-term equity return history, 200+ years of data.
14. **Manias, Panics, and Crashes — Charles Kindleberger.** History of bubbles. Read after you've seen at least one.
15. **Devil Take the Hindmost — Edward Chancellor.** More accessible bubble history.

### Tier 5 — If you want to understand what most retail traders fall for

16. **Reminiscences of a Stock Operator — Edwin Lefèvre.** 1923 fictionalized biography of trader Jesse Livermore. Beautiful, useful, also a cautionary tale (Livermore made and lost multiple fortunes; ended his life by suicide).
17. **Market Wizards — Jack Schwager.** Interviews with successful traders. Read for diversity of approaches; do not try to copy any of them.
18. **Trading in the Zone — Mark Douglas.** Trading psychology. Aimed at active traders.

### What to skip (despite popularity)

- **Rich Dad Poor Dad — Kiyosaki.** Author has been credibly accused of running unregistered investment seminars; the book contains material factual errors and the "rich dad" character may not exist. Motivation, not finance.
- **Anything titled "Get Rich" or with a number in the title promising returns.** Just don't.
- **Most Amazon "Stock Market for Beginners 2026" books.** Self-published, often AI-written, no edit, no signal.

## Section 7 — Technical vs. fundamental analysis (and what the evidence actually says)

You will encounter both schools. Quick definitions:

- **Fundamental analysis** — estimating a company's intrinsic value from its financials, industry position, management, etc. Then buying when price is below intrinsic value.
- **Technical analysis** — predicting future price movements from past price/volume patterns. Charts, moving averages, RSI, MACD, candlestick patterns, etc.

### What the academic evidence says

The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) has three forms:

1. **Weak form:** All past *price* information is already in the price. Implication: technical analysis cannot generate excess returns.
2. **Semi-strong form:** All past price *and public* information is in the price. Implication: fundamental analysis on public info also cannot reliably generate excess returns.
3. **Strong form:** All information including private/insider info is in the price. Implication: even insider trading can't reliably generate excess returns.

Most empirical evidence supports something between weak and semi-strong: markets are *mostly* efficient most of the time, with periodic exploitable inefficiencies. The implications:

- **Pure technical analysis does not have rigorous academic support.** Studies on chart patterns, indicators, and signal-based systems generally find no consistent edge after costs. Weak-form efficiency holds reasonably well.
- **Fundamental analysis can work**, but only for analysts who are genuinely better than the marginal market participant — which is a high bar (you're competing with thousands of full-time professionals). The vast majority of active mutual fund managers, who do this for a living, fail to beat their benchmark over 10+ year horizons.

This is why the index-fund argument is so strong: if markets are mostly efficient, and you can't reliably out-pick professionals, the rational move is to buy the whole market cheaply.

### Where active analysis genuinely helps

- **Asset allocation choices** — your stock/bond mix matters far more than which stocks.
- **Tax efficiency** — placement of assets across taxable vs. tax-advantaged accounts.
- **Behavioral discipline** — having a process you can actually stick to.
- **Niche markets** — small caps, frontier markets, distressed debt where coverage is thin.

### What "BG-Wealth"-style operations exploit

Just to name it: the scam structure your mom is in does not use technical or fundamental analysis. It uses *fabricated* analysis. The "signals" sent in those groups are not real predictions; the platform itself can show whatever P&L it wants because the platform is the counterparty. There's no actual market involved. The "learning" framing is part of the trap. This is worth understanding deeply because if you ever evaluate a platform or "signal service," step one is: *can the entity sending signals also control the displayed price?* If yes, walk away regardless of how good the signals look.

**Do next:**

- Read Fama, "Efficient Capital Markets" (1970) — the original EMH paper. Free, dense.

## Section 8 — Behavioral finance: the biases that will cost you money

Daniel Kahneman won the 2002 Nobel for showing that humans systematically deviate from "rational actor" assumptions in predictable ways. These biases compound especially badly in investing because of feedback delay (you don't know if a decision was good or bad for years).

### The biases that matter most for investors

1. **Loss aversion.** Losses hurt about twice as much as equivalent gains feel good. Causes panic selling at bottoms, holding losers too long ("I'll sell when it gets back to even").
2. **Overconfidence.** Most people rate themselves above average at tasks. In investing, this drives excessive trading, concentrated bets, and ignoring contradictory evidence.
3. **Confirmation bias.** Seeking information that confirms what you already believe. Reading bullish articles about stocks you own; ignoring bearish ones.
4. **Recency bias.** Weighting recent events too heavily. Why people pile into asset classes after they've already had their run.
5. **Anchoring.** Fixating on irrelevant reference points — like your purchase price. The market doesn't care what you paid.
6. **Herding.** Following crowds. Especially dangerous because crowds are most united right before tops and bottoms.
7. **Disposition effect.** Selling winners (to lock in gains) and holding losers (to avoid realizing losses). Tax-inefficient and inverts proper risk management.
8. **Sunk cost fallacy.** "I've put so much into this position, I can't sell now." Past investment is irrelevant; only forward-looking risk/reward matters.
9. **Narrative fallacy.** Constructing stories to explain random events. Markets are largely random short-term; we build stories anyway.
10. **Survivorship bias.** Only seeing winners. Cathie Wood and Bill Ackman get press; the dozens of similarly aggressive managers who blew up don't.

### Defenses

- **Write down your thesis before buying anything.** What do you believe, why, what would prove you wrong? Review quarterly.
- **Pre-commit.** Set rebalancing rules in advance: "Every January, rebalance to target weights." Removes emotion.
- **Automate contributions.** Dollar-cost averaging on a fixed schedule, not "when it feels right."
- **Use a checklist before any trade.** Forces System 2 thinking when System 1 wants to act.
- **Keep a decision journal.** Date, position, thesis, expected outcome. Review honestly. You'll discover which of your own biases dominate.

**Do next:**
- Read *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Chapters 1–10 and 26–28.
- Watch CFA Institute's Kahneman videos (free on their site and YouTube).

## Section 9 — Risk management: the only thing that matters long-term

If there's one section to over-learn, this is it. The professionals who survive 30+ year careers are not the ones with the best ideas. They're the ones whose worst losses didn't end them.

### Core risk concepts

**Position sizing.** No single position should be able to do permanent damage to your portfolio. A common rule: maximum 5% in any single stock, often less. Concentrated portfolios can create wealth (Buffett, Munger, Druckenmiller all concentrated), but they also create most blow-ups.

**Diversification.** Real diversification means owning assets that don't all move together. 50 tech stocks is not diversified. Stocks + long-duration Treasuries + cash + maybe gold + maybe TIPS is diversified.

**Asymmetry.** Look for positions where the upside is meaningfully larger than the downside in expected-value terms. Most retail trades are the opposite — capped upside (sell quickly when up 5%), uncapped downside (hold losers).

**Kelly criterion (don't apply literally, understand conceptually).** The mathematically optimal fraction to bet is `(edge / odds)`. Most retail traders bet far more than Kelly would suggest, even assuming they have edge — which they usually don't.

**Sequence of returns risk.** The order of returns matters enormously near retirement. A 30% drop at age 65 is catastrophic; the same drop at 35 is irrelevant. This is why asset allocation should glide more conservative with age.

**Tail risk.** Markets have "fat tails" — extreme events happen more often than normal distributions predict. 2008, COVID March 2020, 1987. Plan for them. They will happen again.

### The 1-2-3 rule of risk

1. **Size positions so any single one going to zero is annoying, not destructive.**
2. **Diversify so any single asset class going to half is uncomfortable, not destructive.**
3. **Hold enough cash/short-duration bonds that you never have to sell stocks to fund a known expense.**

### Stop losses and active risk management

For a buy-and-hold index investor, stop losses are usually a mistake — they convert volatility into realized losses.

For an active trader, hard stops are non-negotiable. The mistake new traders make is *not having stops* OR *moving stops* once price approaches them. Both are accountability failures dressed up as analysis.

### Leverage

Leverage doesn't create returns, it amplifies them — both directions. Most retail blow-ups involve leverage: margin, options bought without understanding decay, FX with 50:1 brokers, futures, leveraged ETFs held long-term.

The simplest rule: **don't use leverage you don't fully understand**. The slightly more sophisticated rule: **don't use leverage that can force you to sell at a bad time.**

## Section 10 — A reasonable starting plan

Putting it all together, here's a structure that fits the evidence and your situation (mid-30s, employed at NBCU, building QDOT, has cash flow). Adjust to your actual numbers and consult an actual fiduciary advisor before executing anything material.

### Step 1 — The boring foundation (do these in order)

1. Emergency fund: 3–6 months of expenses in HYSA. This is not "investing" — it's the prerequisite that lets you invest without panic-selling.
2. Pay off any debt with rate > ~7%. Credit cards definitely. Student loans maybe.
3. Max employer 401(k) match if NBCU offers one. This is free money; ignoring it is irrational.
4. Open a Roth IRA. 2026 limit: $7,000. Fund it.
5. After match + Roth, additional savings can go to taxable brokerage or back into 401(k).

### Step 2 — Asset allocation (set, then leave alone)

A reasonable starting allocation for a 30-something with stable income and long horizon:

- **70%** Total US Stock Market (e.g., VTI)
- **20%** Total International Stock (e.g., VXUS)
- **10%** Total US Bond Market (e.g., BND)

That's it. Three funds. Rebalance annually. This portfolio has historically returned ~8–10% nominal annualized over 30+ year horizons, with maximum drawdowns in the 40–50% range.

### Step 3 — Optional satellite (max 10–20% of portfolio)

If you want to actively pick stocks, learn options, or experiment, this is the budget. Hard rules:
- Never more than 20% of total invested assets.
- Never any single position more than 5% of total.
- Track every trade with date, thesis, exit plan, actual outcome.
- Review honestly every quarter. If you're underperforming the index after 24 months, kill it.

### Step 4 — Tax efficiency (after the above is in place)

- Bonds in tax-advantaged accounts (interest is taxed as ordinary income).
- US stock index funds in taxable accounts (qualified dividends, low turnover, low capital gains).
- International stock funds in taxable accounts (foreign tax credit recovers some of the foreign withholding).
- Tax-loss harvest in taxable accounts when positions go down.
- Use Roth aggressively early in your career when your tax rate is lower than it will be later.

### Step 5 — Don't touch it

The single highest-value action in long-term investing is doing nothing. Most people destroy their returns by reacting to news, market drops, or "opportunities."
